NVDA · NVIDIA · Watch-only analysis

The best chip company in the most contested layer of the stack.

by Michael S. Lau

NVIDIA makes the accelerators the AI buildout runs on, at 75% gross margin, with revenue more than doubling year over year. It also sells those accelerators to the four companies most actively building replacements for them. The investment question is not whether the numbers are extraordinary. They are. The question is whether the growth rate the multiple assumes can survive its own customers learning to live without it, and whether a stock that looks cheap on trailing growth is cheap at all once that growth decays.

01

The thesis in one page

Watch-only. No position, no price target, no strike-zone bands codified. The thesis is a gate watch, not a buy case.

NVIDIA is the purest play on AI compute and the least defensible fortress in the book at the same time. One quarter of data-center revenue, $107.6 billion, is larger than most semiconductor companies earn in a year, and it grew 110% year over year. The adjusted gross margin is 74.9%. The free cash flow in the same quarter was $43.8 billion. And the four largest buyers of those accelerators, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and OpenAI, are each building their own. The frame for this thesis is that NVIDIA's roughly 90% share of AI accelerators erodes toward 60% as hyperscaler custom silicon takes volume, especially inference at scale. That 90-to-60 is our framing, not a filed forecast, and the erosion can proceed even if NVIDIA executes perfectly, because the growth is fastest exactly where custom silicon is cheapest to deploy. What is working

The numbers are extraordinary

02

The contested middle

NVIDIA sits in the middle of the AI stack and must win its layer every generation. ASML sits at the end of the stack and wins whichever chipmaker wins.

The AI stack has three layers that matter: the machines that make the chips, the chips that do the computing, and the applications that use the computing. ASML owns the first layer outright. The application layer is fragmented across a dozen companies. The middle layer, the accelerator, is where NVIDIA lives, and it is the only layer where the buyers are also the builders of the alternative. Microsoft builds Maia. Google builds TPUs. Amazon builds Trainium. OpenAI has its own program. Broadcom is the arms dealer that designs most of them. None of these companies needs to beat NVIDIA at training to hurt it. They need only to take the inference volume, which is where growth is fastest and where a purpose-built chip is cheapest to justify, and to keep enough training volume in-house to keep improving. INFERENCE from the customer structure

The rock-and-hard-place structure is the core of this thesis. NVIDIA sells chips to customers who are building replacements. If it stays a chip merchant, it feeds the replacement cycle with every sale, because each generation of hyperscaler hardware teaches the buyer more about what it actually needs. If it moves up the stack into full systems, cloud, and software, it competes directly with those same customers, which gives them one more reason to accelerate their own programs. There is no move that does not speed the erosion somewhere. The dodges are real but partial: selling full systems instead of chips raises the bar from silicon to system; the annual cadence from Blackwell to Vera Rubin keeps rivals chasing a moving target; CUDA is the deepest software moat in accelerators; and sovereign-AI buyers are a conflict-free third door, governments that do not build their own chips. The third door is real and it is lumpy and finite. INFERENCE

The erosion frame is roughly 90% AI-accelerator share today, eroding toward 60% as hyperscaler custom silicon takes more volume. That is our framing, not a filed forecast, and it is not a dated measured share. It is the shape of the risk: a dominant share that falls by a third over the custom-silicon cycle, with the pace set by Broadcom design cycles and TSMC capacity, not by anything NVIDIA does wrong. Erosion can proceed even with perfect NVIDIA execution, because growth is fastest in inference at scale on custom silicon. The question the thesis watches is not whether erosion happens. It is how fast, and whether the absolute growth of the market outruns the share loss long enough for the valuation to hold. INFERENCE, our framing, labeled as such

03

The 1-of-1 test, honestly

Verdict: fail. NVIDIA is a 1-of-1 product, not a 1-of-1 company. The distinction is the whole thesis.

The 1-of-1 test asks whether a company is the only one that can do what it does, with competitors chasing from behind under time pressure and a widening moat while the leader compounds market share by orders of magnitude. NVIDIA passes the product version of that test and fails the company version. No one else ships Blackwell-class training systems at this scale today. But the four companies that buy most of them are each building the alternative, and the alternative does not need to be better. It needs to be good enough at inference, cheap enough at scale, and owned outright. A moat that your customers are digging under is not a moat. It is a toll road with an expiry date set by someone else's roadmap. INFERENCE

04

Why others cannot do it (yet)

The bull case, steelmanned first: this is the strongest version of the argument that the erosion never arrives.

Start with what would have to be true for the erosion frame to be wrong. First, CUDA. The software stack is the deepest moat in accelerators: a decade of libraries, developer habit, and tooling that makes switching a rewrite, not a swap. A rival chip that matches the silicon still loses if the software is not there. Second, systems. NVIDIA no longer sells chips. It sells NVLink-connected systems, networking, and reference designs, so a competitor must match the system, not the die. Third, cadence. Blackwell to Vera Rubin to Rubin Ultra on a yearly rhythm means a rival is always chasing last year's platform while the next one ships. Fourth, scale. A $107.6 billion data-center quarter buys supply priority at TSMC and in memory that no challenger can match, and the CFO's own guide says fiscal 2028 growth is supply-constrained, which is the opposite of a demand problem. Fifth, sovereign AI. Governments that do not build chips buy NVIDIA, and that demand does not erode with hyperscaler roadmaps. INFERENCE, the steelmanned bull case

Each of these is real, and each has a limit. CUDA is a switching cost, not a lock: hyperscalers write their own stacks, and inference workloads are narrower than training, so the rewrite is smaller where the volume is moving. Systems raise the bar, but the hyperscalers are system builders themselves, and the more NVIDIA moves up the stack, the more directly it competes with them. Cadence keeps rivals chasing, but it also compresses NVIDIA's own margins: the Q4 guide to 71-72% gross margin, down from 74.9% delivered in Q3, is the cost of moving that fast while memory prices spike. Scale buys supply priority, but supply commitments jumped from $119 billion to $279 billion in Q2 and to $390 billion by end-Q3, mostly memory procurement, which is a liability if demand ever pauses. Sovereign AI is a real third door, but it is lumpy, political, and finite, and it cannot replace hyperscaler volume. The bull case is strong. It is also the case for a company that must win its layer every generation, against customers who set the pace. INFERENCE

01

Custom silicon takes the inference mix

Microsoft Maia, Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, OpenAI's program, largely via Broadcom. The erosion is fastest in inference at scale, where a purpose-built chip is cheapest to justify and where CUDA matters least. This is the threat the whole thesis turns on. Third-party benchmarks now put numbers on it: SemiAnalysis's AgentX results (November 2026) have Google's TPUv7 beating Blackwell on agentic inference performance per dollar, and Meta is deploying TPUv7 in production. Anthropic says its Mythos tooling ported Claude to AMD MI355X GPUs in about three days, against six to twelve months under traditional methods, but the claim narrows to prompt processing for one model, is Anthropic's own from IPO marketing, and stays unverified; Nvidia remains one of the four chip families in Anthropic's pool. Google extended the same threat's breadth on Nov 9, adding TPU 8tx for secure, confidential inference and TPU 8i Max for massive context windows and the highest compute density per rack, four TPU systems in one year, a first; the variants are reconfigured versions of the eighth-generation design, not new chips, and no customer win, deployment disclosure or share-shift figure was disclosed. NVIDIA's counter on the distribution leg is its agreed $12.93 billion acquisition of Hugging Face, the hub where open models are discovered and deployed, which would defend inference share because model choice is made before silicon choice. The deal was agreed Sept 3, 2026 and is expected to close in the first half of 2027, but the Justice Department asked in October 2026 whether it would let NVIDIA capture a fundamental AI distribution point, putting a regulatory overhang on NVIDIA's largest-ever acquisition, with no finding or charge and the platform pledged to stay open. VENDOR CLAIM (hyperscaler programs are public; the pace is our inference) The inference challengers can also raise at step-ups on promise: Bloomberg reported Nov 6 that Nvidia-backed Groq is seeking a Series B at an $8 billion pre-money valuation, about 2.5 times its August mark, while a separate report said Groq cut more than $1 billion from its 2025 revenue projections - capital formation is not deployment, and the raise has no named amount or lead investor. NVIDIA's answer to the same threat is absorption rather than exclusion: at AI Day Seoul on Nov 9, NVIDIA, Rebellions and SK Telecom agreed to connect Rebellions' next-generation R200 NPUs to NVIDIA's platform via NVLink Fusion in MGX racks, with SK Telecom deploying the mixed systems and R200 systems expected ready in 2028. It is a memorandum of understanding, not a product launch or purchase commitment, with no performance figures, volumes, prices or revenue terms disclosed; a rival chip attaching to NVIDIA's fabric is the full-systems pillar working as designed, while conceding that custom silicon is coming. Nov 23 addendum, Amazon buys Decart tech to sharpen Trainium: Amazon signed an agreement Nov 23 to buy the technology behind Decart Optimization Stack and hire its founders and engineering team, with Bloomberg reporting about $7.5 billion; Amazon says it will optimize inference and custom silicon on Trainium as it prepares to sell the chips externally, a direct custom-silicon challenge to Nvidia. Supporting evidence for the erosion leg, threat 01; no needle, pillar, probability, verdict or sizing change.

02

AI capex digestion

The buildout is the largest capital program in history, and the buyers are funding it with debt and third-party capital. If hyperscaler capex pauses to digest, NVIDIA's growth falls off a cliff that no moat softens: the quarter that grew 103% can print flat just as fast. The Nov 5 selloff, down 9.26% on the day and 24.09% over the month, is the market pricing exactly this risk. The permitting leg behind that risk is now quantified: Wood Mackenzie counts more than 400 state and local moratorium actions since January 2026, against 45 across all of 2023-2025, and voters favored restrictions in 40 of 41 local data-center measures on Nov 3. JPMorgan Asset Management's read is 'more bark than bite, so far': only one of 16 states considering legislation has passed a bill, and capacity under construction grew nearly 50% in the first half of 2026. The exposure concentrates in the 2028-plus pipeline, where the fiscal 2028 growth guide sits. One workaround is already on the table: NVIDIA has proposed to Apollo converting some of Brightspeed's 130-plus telecom buildings into small AI data centers across up to 20 states, with about 1 gigawatt of power by NVIDIA's estimate, for Vera Rubin inference servers from Q1 2027. It is a proposal, not an agreement, with no confirmed customers, and at 130 buildings it is small next to the carrier central-office stock, but it shows the buildout finding already-permitted, already-powered capacity instead of waiting out moratoriums. Generational churn cuts the other way too: Bloomberg reported Nov 6 that Microsoft is winding down its 2023 deal to rent AI computing from Oracle, declining to renew because the Oracle servers run older-generation Nvidia chips that no longer meet its specifications - rented capacity goes obsolete fast, which is why the digestion risk bites older hardware first. The deal size was never disclosed. INFERENCE

04

China: a licensed trickle, then zero

Q3 carried $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data-center revenue to China, then Nvidia halted further shipments; the Q4 outlook assumes no China data-center revenue. The only stated path back is conditional and distant: Trump said Nov 18 he could allow Blackwell sales to China in two to three years, once newer chips have far surpassed them, a statement rather than a policy change. That is the floor of the China story, not the ceiling: there is nothing left to lose, but also nothing to gain without a licensing path, and any tightening elsewhere in the export regime hits the sovereign and supply story instead. Approvals are not just absent but delayed: Reuters reported in November 2026 that as many as 600,000 NVIDIA AI chips lie stockpiled in Hong Kong awaiting shipment to mainland China as Beijing delays approvals (single-source, not a company disclosure). VERIFIED (Q3 FY2027 press release, Nov 17, 2026) Chinese labs are also reaching Blackwell through the remote-access gap: export controls restrict shipment of the chips to China, but renting capacity at overseas data centers was not broadly controlled, and the House passed the Remote Access Security Act in January 2026 to close that gap, with the bill pending in the Senate. Commerce closed the gap by rule on Nov 16, 2026, with an interim final rule requiring a license where a data center provides more than 12 million aggregate TPP of compute to China-, Macau- or D:5-headquartered firms; the effective date is disputed (90 days after issuance per one account, on Federal Register publication per another), so the licensing bite is not yet in force. The rule is already biting in practice: Moonshot AI scrapped a $150 million contract to rent Blackwell Ultra B300 chips at an Indonesian data center after the rule issued, having agreed to prepay about 50 percent to secure the capacity for training its next model, Kimi K4. The Washington Post reports the rule was written by and at the direction of Treasury and the National Security Council, not Commerce. A documented case of the leakage: a PLA-linked university on the US Entity List since 2001 bought two H200 chips for about $123,000 through a domestic reseller and holds five rental contracts for Nvidia-powered compute; no violation was established against Nvidia itself. CNN's review of nearly 300 Chinese university procurement records, published Nov 19, widened the documented leakage to at least six Entity List universities, including September-October 2026 buys: Harbin Institute of Technology bought an A100 for about $7,000 and Northwestern Polytechnical University bought four H200 chips for about $240,000; delivery was not verified in each case. A further reported diversion conduit: The Information reported Nov 12 that companies connected to Alice Huang took part in DeepSeek's second funding round (about $15.8B, closed October), pitched as able to supply restricted Blackwell chips, through Litong Inc., said to have imported 240,000 Nvidia GPUs into China since 2023; whether the chip-supply pledge was fulfilled is unconfirmed, and Alice Huang holds no direct stake in Litong. Another quantified datapoint: the Wall Street Journal reported, from records it reviewed, that Megaspeed brokered about $12 billion in Nvidia chip orders for Chinese companies between March 2024 and June 2025, though delivered quantities were not verified, and gave Alibaba remote access to Nvidia servers in Malaysia. The New York Times reported Nov 19 that Alibaba was the customer and financial backer behind Megaspeed's own purchases of more than $4 billion in Nvidia chips over several years, and that Alibaba co-founded an AI lab with strong ties to China's military, whose researchers work on automating cyberattacks and simulating war games; Alibaba has denied allegations of military support. Forensic weight analysis added a technical leg to the diversion evidence: former OpenAI researcher Will DePue found six leading Chinese open-weight models, including DeepSeek-V4 and Moonshot Kimi K3, bear fingerprints of training on export-banned Blackwell chips, with the largest runs estimated at roughly 2,000 to 3,000 Blackwell GPUs each. The report is explicit that the fingerprints show access to the hardware, not where the chips were, how they were obtained, or whether export controls were violated, and offshore data-center rental remains a possible route. NVIDIA is policing the channel itself: the Financial Times reported it told cloud partners to direct at least 51 percent of services to non-Chinese customers on new contracts, and Bloomberg reported it requires new Southeast Asia cloud partners to enroll in Fleet Intelligence location tracking to keep Nvidia Cloud Partner status and has removed half of the Asian companies it had previously pre-approved for chip purchases. The system does not auto-share location data with NVIDIA or the US government. CNBC reported Nov 17 that Nvidia told customers it will not, for now, sell chips to buyers renting more than 50% of their GPUs to Chinese companies, and warned that proposed remote-access legislation could expose buyers to US legal action; Nvidia has not published the policy. Even the legal channel is supply-capped: Bloomberg reported Nov 4 that Chinese AI and cloud firms including Alibaba, Tencent, and ByteDance have ordered about 6 million export-compliant RTX PRO 5500 cards, worth about $70 billion, but expect to receive only about 1 percent over the next four months, and NVIDIA has indicated it can produce no more than 3 million globally in 2027. The card is a Blackwell-based workstation GPU that complies with US export rules, so the demand is real, but orders are not deliveries. And the licensing channel itself is porous: the New York Times reported Nov 7 that Commerce cleared roughly two dozen Chinese firms to handle H200 chips, including New Matrix, a 17-month-old firm cleared to assemble H200s into servers whose listed Beijing office was empty and whose owners are tied to Wise Road Capital, a fund the US blacklisted in 2024. No diversion evidence was reported and neither Commerce nor NVIDIA commented - it is a vetting failure, not a proven diversion. The scale of the licensed channel is now quantified: the House Foreign Affairs Committee disclosed Nov 19 that Commerce had approved 879,868 Nvidia and AMD chips for Chinese companies from December 2025 to May 2026, out of 3,444,160 requested, including a 445,000-H200 request for Tencent; every approved entry was still unshipped as of May 11, 2026, and it is not clear how many later shipped. A further reported diversion case sits in the same grey area: Bloomberg reported, from Beijing regulatory filings, that Moonshot AI signed August contracts to rent 32 Supermicro servers with Nvidia B300 GPUs, valued at about $64.8 million and located in China; Moonshot disputes the filings' accuracy, says it has no relationship with the server owner, and Nvidia says the parties involved are not its customers, so no breach is established. A Reuters review of Chinese contracts and disclosures identified more than $3 billion in Chinese computing projects built or planned around US-restricted Nvidia GPUs, though the projects have not been linked to Megaspeed and the figure covers buildings, power and infrastructure, not chips alone. Bloomberg reported, from documents it reviewed, that a Chinese company founded by Alice Huang procured smuggled Nvidia AI chips installed at a Shanghai data center with links to Megaspeed; the filings do not establish her personal involvement, and she sold her shares in the firm in 2017. A separate Taiwan case reaches Nvidia directly: Keelung prosecutors indicted a Taiwan-based Nvidia manager surnamed Chang in August, calling him the core figure of a network that shipped 74 servers containing Nvidia B300 chips to China, with 56 more seized by the Coast Guard in May; Nvidia pressed Taipei officials that Chang should not be under investigation and asked TSMC for advice, which TSMC declined, and says its own investigation found no indication Chang knew of the diversion. How the growth compounds

Four engines, in order of how much of the growth they explain.

First, data-center scale. Data-center revenue of $107.6 billion in Q3 FY2027, up 110% year over year, is about 93% of total revenue. The growth is the story, and the constraint is supply: the CFO guided fiscal 2028 revenue growth of about 75% and called it supply-constrained. When supply is the constraint, every incremental wafer, memory chip, and networking port converts directly into revenue. When demand becomes the constraint, the same machine stops just as fast. Advanced packaging is part of that constraint: Reuters reported in November 2026 that NVIDIA is providing billions of dollars in financing to expand chip-on-wafer-on-substrate (CoWoS) packaging capacity at Siliconware Precision Industries, a unit of ASE Technology, a second source beyond TSMC. NVIDIA confirmed the SPIL financing itself on Nov 17, alongside expanded supply-chain commitments: Kinsus will grow ABF substrate capacity dedicated to NVIDIA by more than 3.5x (production in H2 2027, targeted Q3 2028), and Amkor gets nearly $3B in multiyear agreements including prepayments for advanced-packaging expansion in Arizona and South Korea. The commitments are supply-side de-bottlenecking for the guided growth. VERIFIED (Q3 FY2027 press release and call transcript, Nov 17, 2026)

Second, the platform step-up. Blackwell to Vera Rubin is not a chip swap, it is a system replacement, and Vera Rubin delivered nearly $20 billion in its launch quarter, about 19% of data-center revenue. Each platform generation re-prices the installed base upward and resets the comparison for rivals, who must match the system, not the die. The cost is the margin: the Q4 guide to 71-72% is the price of moving this fast while memory inflates. The platform step-up also extends beyond data-center AI: NVIDIA announced Rubin HPC at SC26 on Nov 16, a GPU with more than 50,000 CUDA cores for science and engineering workloads, expected in 2028, so no near-term revenue read. VENDOR CLAIM (earnings-call transcript)

Third, networking and software. NVLink and the networking stack turn single accelerators into systems, and the software layer, CUDA plus the inference and deployment tooling, is what makes the system sticky. This is the closest thing to a recurring engine in the thesis, and it is also the engine most exposed to the inference mix shift, where narrower workloads need less of the stack. INFERENCE

Fourth, sovereign AI. Governments that do not build their own chips buy NVIDIA, and that demand does not follow hyperscaler roadmaps. It is a real third door and it is lumpy, political, and finite. It widens the customer base without widening the moat, and it cannot replace hyperscaler volume if the erosion accelerates. INFERENCE

The saturation framing: growth compounds while wafer and memory supply are the constraint and hyperscaler capex keeps rising. It stops compounding when either the capex cycle digests or custom silicon takes enough of the inference mix that the incremental dollar goes to Broadcom's customers instead. The first sign of saturation is not a revenue miss. It is the growth rate bending toward the guided 70% and then past it, while the forward PEG stays above 1.5. SemiAnalysis's Nov 13 forecast revision points the same way: AI accelerator spend cut 19-40% for 2027-2030, about $1.6T lower cumulatively, with Nvidia bearing the bulk on lower chip pricing, though spending still grows every year under the revised model; it is a forecast, not a filed print, so the deceleration needle stays watching. That combination is the needle this thesis watches. INFERENCE

Valuation and the cheapness question

The question that opened this thesis: the trailing PEG is low and the Rule of 40 is great, so is it cheap value add? The short answer is that the trailing numbers flatter, and the forward numbers do not.

Start with the numbers as they print. The trailing PEG is 0.20, from a trailing P/E of 25.30 divided by trailing EPS growth of 125.36%. The forward PEG is about 1.57. The forward P/E prints disagree by definition: about 12.9 on a next-twelve-months basis (TipRanks, Nov 2) and 14.26 in a Zacks article (Nov 3), against 21.73 to 22.01 on Zacks' own F1 quote page. The Rule of 40 on the Q2 print is 128: 106% revenue growth plus a 22.1% free-cash-flow margin ($21,341 million on $96,221 million of revenue). On the Q3 print the score is about 141: 103% revenue growth plus a 37.8% free-cash-flow margin ($43.8 billion on $115.8 billion of revenue). Every one of those numbers is real. The question is what each one assumes. VERIFIED (FinanceCharts Nov 4, Zacks Nov 2-3, TipRanks Nov 2, Q2 FY2027 press release; Q3 FY2027 press release, Nov 17, 2026)

The trailing PEG is a rearview mirror. It divides today's price by a growth rate of 125% that cannot repeat, because the comp was a quarter when the AI buildout was a fraction of its current size. A PEG below 1.0 is supposed to mean cheap relative to growth, but the growth in the denominator is already history: the company itself guides fiscal 2028 revenue growth to about 75%, and the deceleration is the point. Divide the same price by 75% growth and the PEG is about 70% higher than the trailing print. The forward PEG near 1.57 is the honest growth-adjusted read, and it says fairly priced for a company growing 75%, not cheap. The forward P/E disagreement is the same story told twice: the low print uses a forward year that already includes the growth, the high print uses the fiscal year in progress. This thesis does not rest on a single multiple, and neither should the cheapness claim. INFERENCE from the arithmetic

The Rule of 40 tells the same story from the other side. A score of 128 is extraordinary, and it is also entirely a growth score: 106 points of it are revenue growth, 22 points are cash margin. The growth leg is guided to about 75% in fiscal 2028, so the score decays by roughly 28 points from the Q3 print before anything else changes. A Rule-of-40 score that depends on a non-repeatable growth comp is not a margin of safety. It is a snapshot of a peak. The margin leg is the one that has to carry the score as growth decays, and the margin leg is the one the company just guided down, 74.9% delivered in Q3 to 71-72% guided in Q4. INFERENCE

So is it cheap value add? On trailing numbers, yes, optically. On forward numbers, no: a forward PEG near 1.57 for a company whose own customers are building its replacements, whose growth is guided from 103% to about 75%, and whose margin is guided down about three points, is fair value for the risk, not a bargain. The value-add case would need one of three things: growth holding above the guide while the multiple stays compressed, evidence the erosion has stalled, or a price that pays for the erosion in advance. At $150.96, down a further 2.89% on Nov 18, the market has started to pay for it. It has not finished. No price target is set and no strike-zone bands are codified: this is a watch-only thesis, and a band would imply a buy case the 1-of-1 verdict does not support. Downside floor

There is no price floor here. The floor is earnings power, and it is thinner than the growth story suggests.

Net cash of about $23.6 billion (aggregator-reported: total cash $62.47 billion against total debt $38.86 billion) is about 0.7% of a roughly $3.6 trillion market cap. It bounds nothing. The buyback is the only mechanical bid: about $227.3 billion of authorization remaining, to be used through FY2028, plus a $6 billion quarterly dividend. The founder overhang sits alongside it: Huang keeps roughly 812 million shares and remains the largest individual owner, and as a former insider under 5% he can generally sell without a public filing once a roughly 90-day Rule 144 affiliate window passes, with the legal cap estimated at about 600 million shares per quarter; no sale has been reported. A buyback supports a price only while the buyer believes in the growth, and the quarter that grew 103% returned a record $28.1 billion to shareholders. The floor is the data-center earnings engine itself: $107.6 billion of quarterly revenue at 74.9% adjusted gross margin, with $43.8 billion of free cash flow in a recovering working-capital quarter. If the growth decelerates faster than the guided 75%, the multiple compresses and the buyback cannot hold the price, because the buyback is funded by the same earnings that are decelerating.
