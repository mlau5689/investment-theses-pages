01 · Bottom line

Executive verdict

The strategic thesis holds up: rare-earth magnets can't be engineered out of enough high-value uses, so qualified non-China supply will become scarce. But on the evidence so far, USAR is a time-to-capacity option whose next three years depend on Brazilian heavy rare earths and yttrium prices, not on humanoid robots or Terafab.

About 42% of Serra Verde's modeled revenue has no price floor. 02 · The thesis in my words

Why I am paying attention before the revenue arrives

My thesis is not simply that the world needs more rare-earth ore. It is that demand recognition can move in months while mines, separation plants, alloy lines, magnet factories and customer qualifications take years.

In an AI and national-security arms race, the United States cannot accept a single-country choke point in the permanent magnets, heavy rare earths, semiconductor materials and defense components needed for physical AI. Whoever already owns feedstock, metallization, manufacturing and customer qualification when the shortage becomes obvious has a time advantage that late entrants cannot purchase quickly.

This is the same pick-and-shovel logic I use elsewhere: own the scarce enabling layer before the market fully recognizes that it is scarce. I expect engineers to remove rare earths wherever possible. The thesis therefore rests on the uses where compactness, heat resistance, power density, precision, reliability, qualification and security of supply make substitution uneconomic or operationally unacceptable.

What I am underwriting

  • A strategic premium for qualified non-China supply.
  • Persistent bottlenecks in separation, metals, alloys and high-grade magnets.
  • Defense and physical-AI demand arriving before new Western supply can scale.
  • Vertical integration as a time and qualification advantage, if it becomes commercially profitable.

What I am not underwriting

  • Terafab proximity as a valuation driver.
  • Solar panels as a direct rare-earth demand engine.
  • Rocket volume or orbital data centers as near-term tonnage.
  • Every announced tonne of capacity achieving yield, qualification and utilization.

04 · Demand from tech projections

Robots × kilograms: bottom-up demand

I built demand up from technology forecasts instead of rare-earth industry studies. Published estimates put humanoid magnet content at 2–4.5 kg NdFeB per robot. I use 2.5–3 kg as the midpoint, with 3.5 kg as the high end. A magnet is mostly iron: 3 kg of NdFeB holds about 0.9 kg of NdPr and only 35–79 g of Dy.

What this means

  • Early capacity has to be filled by defense, EV, industrial motors and procurement mandates.

A subtle point: robots are mainly a light-REE story

Robot joints mostly use H/SH magnet grades, which run cooler than EV traction motors. With grain-boundary diffusion they need little Dy/Tb. FAI estimates EVs already use heavy rare earths equal to about 26M robots a year. So robots support demand for NdPr, but they weaken the case for owning USAR's heavy rare earths because of robots. Keep three separate models: NdFeB tonnes, NdPr, and Dy/Tb.

05 · Conservatism test

Where rare earths are truly hard to replace

"Indispensable" here means hard to substitute within a qualified design and its performance limits, not physically impossible to design around. Ranked by how hard it is to engineer them out:

1. Defense and aerospace

Missile fin actuators, seekers, radar and TWTs, sonar, and fighter actuators and generators. SmCo and high-coercivity Dy/Tb NdFeB, plus YSZ thermal-barrier coatings. Buyers are price-insensitive and qualification is locked in. The Pentagon puts ~900 lb of rare-earth material in an F-35 and ~9,200 lb in a Virginia-class submarine.

2. Humanoid joints, cobots and drones

Ferrite has about a tenth of NdFeB's energy product, and wound-field motors don't shrink well to 30–70 small joints. This is one of the few mass-market uses where the thesis is right, though 5–10 years early. The Army's SkyFoundry drone magnet award went to Vulcan, not USAR.

3. Semiconductor equipment

Wafer stages, vacuum robots, turbomolecular pumps (SmCo), yttria plasma-resistant coatings, and ceria polishing. Tonnage is small but value is high. This is where Terafab really touches rare earths, and it runs through yttrium.

4. Optical and laser

Erbium in EDFA amplifiers (the 1,550 nm window), plus Nd, Yb and Tm lasers. These are essential to specific designs, not to every data-center link. The material mass involved is tiny.

5. Premium EV traction and offshore direct-drive wind

Advantaged, but substitution is actively under way. The IEA notes Japan cut rare-earth demand 30% after 2010, and recycling could cut primary needs by up to 35% by 2050.

6. Data-center fans, pumps and HDDs

Substitutable at the margin. Ferrite-based cooling motor and pump products are already being sold.

06 · Supply side

The real bottleneck is the middle of the chain

Ore is not the scarce part. The scarce part is the chain from ore to qualified magnet. China holds ~91% of refined magnet-REE output and ~94% of sintered magnets, and its refining share only falls to about 70–73% by 2035. The IEA says planned magnet production outside China reaches only about a third of expected mine output by 2035. The US was 100% import-reliant for heavy-REE compounds and metals in 2025.

Surviving China pricing

MP Materials: the benchmark

MP produced 840 t of NdPr in Q2 2026 and sold 1,006 t, on $108.5M of revenue, and guides to more than 1,000 t in Q3. Its planned ~10,000 t of US magnet capacity (Independence 3,000 t plus 10X 7,000 t, commissioning 2028) comes with a 10-year DoD NdPr floor of $110/kg and 100% offtake for 10X. That is significant, but it doesn't close the gap. Total US NdFeB demand is ~48 kt, most of it inside imported goods, and MP still needs heavy-REE inputs.

Do the floodgates open in 2028?

No. 2028 is when plants start commissioning, not when they reach full output. The underrated bear case is temporary Western overcapacity: about 48 kt a year of announced US magnet capacity by 2030, up from ~2 kt in 2025, while customers qualify new suppliers slowly and Chinese magnets are priced off NdPr at ~$97–110/kg. Price floors, DFARS, tariffs and 45X credits exist because the market alone won't keep these plants full.

Oct 19 addendum, Jan 2027 rule expands to the full chain: verified against the DFARS and statutory text (DFARS 225.7018, operative clause 252.225-7052): a prohibition already exists today at the melt/produce stage, and on January 1, 2027 it expands to the entire chain, covering material mined, refined, separated, melted, or produced in China, Russia, North Korea, or Iran, from NdFeB mining through finished magnets. A harder regulatory demand floor for ex-China magnet supply than the thesis's earlier shorthand implied.

08 · The key stress test

Serra Verde's yttrium exposure

Management's run-rate EBITDA target of $550–650M assumes ~4,400 t a year of TREO (after removing lanthanum) at a basket price of ~$190/kg, which is ~$836M of revenue. By value, the basket is 42% Y, 22% NdPr, 19% Dy and 13% Tb. NdPr, Dy and Tb have floors ($110, $575 and $2,050/kg). Yttrium has no floor. Oct 21 addendum, yttrium supply crunch verified: the scarcity premium this thesis assumed the US-China truce would deflate is instead severe and tightening. The Financial Times reports Japan is months away from running out of yttrium, and a Japanese industry ministry official (via AFP, Oct 21) confirms exports have "stalled not only to Japan but worldwide, causing a supply-demand crunch and soaring prices." Argus pricing puts yttrium oxide at ~$1,150/kg outside China against ~$9/kg inside it, a 179-fold gap built in two years, and Chinese customs data shows exports to Japan down 97.7% in Jan-May 2026. This is spot/export-market evidence, not a USAR contract, but it moves the yttrium leg toward the base case's partial-premium assumption and away from the Chinese-domestic floor case. It also cuts against the truce narrative carried in this document since Oct 15: the truce covers licensed exports at the policy level, while China's controls are biting hard in practice. The licensed-normalization de-rating trigger stands, the premium is policy-made, not cost-made, and a grand bargain would still collapse it.

11 · Policy and environment

China risk cuts both ways; ESG is both a moat and a drag

China scenarios

  • Tighter controls (the MOFCOM Notice 61 pause ends Nov 10): support Western premiums and urgency, but can also hold back feedstock and equipment.
  • Licensed normalization (grand bargain): the most likely trigger for a de-rating, because it hits unprotected yttrium.
  • Split pricing: cheap Chinese domestic prices alongside expensive Western material. A single "global price" is misleading.
  • The Sept 24–25 Trump–Xi summit only extended the truce to January 2027. Chinese exporters are already refusing some US-bound shipments.

Environmental constraint

  • Separation chemistry, radioactive residues and tailings lengthen permitting and raise Western costs relative to China.
  • Serra Verde is exposed to Brazilian licensing, political scrutiny and community opposition.
  • Round Top's heap leach adds reagent cost and radioactive-waste handling.
  • Once a plant is running and compliant, those same barriers deter copycats. Defense and chip buyers pay for traceable, compliant supply, and price floors put a value on that.

Strategic alignment: Tier 1, the state as demand-maker

This is the formal grade on what the thesis already assumes. Strategic alignment measures how directly the US government is committed to a company's success: through its spending, its contracts, its regulatory grants, or its structural dependence on what the company provides. USAR grades Tier 1, the strongest grade, because the state is not merely a customer here but the demand-maker. The market for domestic mine-to-magnet supply exists at strategic scale because defense procurement law excludes the Chinese chain: the DFARS full-chain NdFeB ban expands on January 1, 2027, and the base case in section 12 already assumes the contractual floors hold for NdPr, Dy and Tb behind that exclusion. Holds

Alignment of this kind turbo-charges support: demand durability across administrations, federal financing paths, and a policy ratchet that tends to tighten rather than loosen as Chinese leverage grows. The caveat is symmetric and load-bearing. A government-guaranteed market does not guarantee company execution. The ban creates the floor; Serra Verde's ramp, Stillwater's qualification, and the funding path decide whether USAR collects it. Alignment breaks ties between fortresses. Orbital data centers: can cooling work?

Yes, but only by radiating heat. With no convection in vacuum, heat has to be moved from cold plates through pumped loops or heat pipes to large radiators. Radiator area and mass set the limit, not magnet supply. Passive heat pipes also reduce the number of motors. This is upside only, with no value assigned.

14 · Analyst's overlay

Pressure-tested agreements and divergences

The analyst agrees with the strategic scarcity thesis, but narrows the parts that deserve valuation credit today. These are analytical overlays, not buy or sell recommendations.

The time moat is real, and it cuts both ways

Demand can become obvious faster than supply can respond. That gives early builders a window, but USAR itself must survive the same multi-year build. MP is roughly two to three years ahead on the operating and qualification clock. The durable moat is time-to-build plus government price backstops, not time alone.

Check whether the rumor is already priced

USAR came public around a $464M listing valuation, ran toward roughly $44 a share, and sits near $14.46 in this snapshot (it later filled at $10.00 on Oct 15, 2026; see the buy-price rerun in Valuation). The council's base case calls the shares roughly fairly priced here. Material upside therefore requires something closer to bull-case execution, not merely the strategic narrative becoming popular.

Orbital compute gets no valuation credit

In vacuum, heat leaves through radiation. A first-principles estimate at practical chip-coolant temperatures puts 1 MW of compute at roughly 650–1,200 m² of radiator panel before full-system mass and redundancy. Only single-GPU-class data-center hardware has flown, and economic MW-scale orbital compute before 2030 rates low. This challenges the near-term orbital-compute leg of the SpaceX thesis and belongs as a 2030s option.

The non-substitutable floor is about one-third

The conservative must-have core is roughly 65–70 Kt of today's market, about 28–30%. The world will engineer rare earths out where size and performance permit. The thesis does not need every application; it needs the qualified, compact, hot, precise and defense-sensitive core to keep growing faster than protected supply.

Rocket cadence is strategically important, not volumetrically important

Even a deliberately generous 50 kg of magnets per vehicle and 1,000 Starship flights per year implies only about 50 tonnes annually. That is immaterial to global magnet tonnage. Rockets matter because failures are unacceptable and non-China qualification is valuable, not because launch cadence transforms demand.

Vertical integration is an advantage only when each link works

USAR's Brazil-France-UK-US chain improves traceability and access, but today it is assembled integration, not one proven commercial flywheel. Feedstock, separation, metallurgy, magnets, qualification and working-capital transfer must all function. Until then, integration increases both strategic value and capital burden.

Where the analyst agrees with my thesis

  • Qualified Western capacity will remain scarce.
  • The middle of the chain is the real bottleneck.
  • Defense, aerospace, semiconductor tooling and robotics create hard-to-substitute demand.
  • Long lead times favor assets already under construction.
  • USAR's most differentiated edge is heavy-REE feedstock plus metallization.

Where the analyst is more conservative

  • Humanoids are a 2030s demand driver, not the bridge to 2028.
  • Data centers and solar do not justify broad rare-earth demand claims.
  • MP owns the stronger near-term operating lead.
  • Yttrium pricing and cash access dominate the next three years.
  • The current price already reflects part of the rumor.

Open questions that would change my thesis

Economics and cash

  • What yttrium realization is embedded in the $550–650M Serra Verde EBITDA target?
  • What is EBITDA and free cash flow if yttrium is 50% lower and separated oxides are delayed one year?
  • How much Serra Verde cash can move to the parent after DFC covenants, debt service and local capex?
  • Which grants and loans have actually been drawn, and what equity contribution is required before each draw?
  • What is the fully funded path through 2028 under a six- and twelve-month delay?

Operations and qualification

  • Are Stillwater shipments paid, repeat, qualified orders with positive product gross margin?
  • Can Serra Verde show the chain from tonnes produced to invoice, collection and upstreamable cash?
  • Who is the binding anchor customer for magnets, metals and yttrium?
  • What yields, recovery rates and capex does the Round Top DFS prove?
  • Which separation step is owned, which is contracted, and which remains dependent on a minority partner?

Pricing and policy

  • Is there a contracted yttrium floor or strategic buyer?
  • How would the offtake behave if Chinese exports normalize and Western premiums narrow?
  • How much of planned capacity is protected by enforceable procurement rules or price floors?
  • Do DFARS rules create actual orders or only eligibility?
  • What volume is exposed to unprotected commodity pricing?

Shareholder outcome
